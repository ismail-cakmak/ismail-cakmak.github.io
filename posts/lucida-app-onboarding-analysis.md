Lucida is already one of the successful consumer apps coming out of Turkey. That is why I think it is worth looking at closely. When a product reaches this level, small UX decisions start to matter more: the first screen, the onboarding questions, the homepage hierarchy, the avatar, the paywall timing, and the way the user is guided into speaking.

I analyzed Lucida’s onboarding and homepage because AI speaking apps are not only about technology. The user also needs to feel safe, motivated, and clear about what to do next. Speaking a new language can be stressful, so the product experience has to reduce that stress from the first session.

In this post, I look at where Lucida already works well, where the experience feels a bit unclear, and how it compares with apps like Learna, Lingo AI, and Pingo. The goal is not to criticize the product from outside, but to understand how a strong app can become even stronger with a clearer homepage, a more emotional onboarding flow, and a better speaking practice experience.

# 1. The onboarding and the main app feel slightly different

One of the first things that stands out is the difference between Lucida’s onboarding UI and the rest of the app.

The app itself has a more gamified, roadmap-based feeling. It feels closer to products like Duolingo, where users move through lessons step by step. But the onboarding screens feel more plain and separate from that world.

![Lucida daily dedication onboarding screen compared with Learna welcome screen](posts/image/lucida-app-onboarding-analysis/1.png)

This creates a small disconnect.

When a user finishes onboarding and lands on the homepage, it should feel like they have entered the same product they were just introduced to. Learna does this better. Even its first onboarding screen already feels close to its homepage style. The buttons, avatar, colors, and layout all support the same product feeling.

For Lucida, the onboarding could use more of the app’s own visual language which is duolingo style. This would make the first experience feel more unified.

# 2. The homepage has too many competing choices

Lucida’s homepage has a cognitive load problem.

There are many tappable elements on the screen: the “Casual Talk” button, several roadmap lesson buttons, and the “Learn with Songs” button. The issue is not only the number of options. The bigger issue is that these options visually compete with each other.

For a new user, “Casual Talk” looks like the main call-to-action. It is placed strongly and feels more emphasized than the roadmap items. Because of that, a user may think this is the next lesson they are supposed to take.

![](posts/image/lucida-app-onboarding-analysis/2.png)

But if the product wants users to follow a learning path, then the next roadmap item should be the clearest action on the screen.

Learna and Duolingo handle this better. Their homepages reduce the number of choices. The user does not need to think much. The screen almost says: “Tap here. This is your next step.”

Lucida should aim for the same feeling. The homepage should answer one question immediately:

**What should I do now?**

A few possible improvements:

The next lesson should be the most visually emphasized item.

Casual Talk should either be clearly separated as a secondary action or positioned as part of the learning journey.

The number of visible roadmap items could be reduced.

Secondary features like songs can stay, but they should not compete with the main path.

# 3. The avatar can make Lucida feel like a real speaking buddy

For a speaking app, the avatar is not just decoration. It is part of the promise.

When users open the app and see an avatar waiting for them, the product feels more alive. It gives the feeling that someone is ready to talk. This matters because speaking practice is emotional. Users may feel judged, embarrassed, or unsure.

A visible avatar can reduce that tension.

![](posts/image/lucida-app-onboarding-analysis/3.png)

Learna uses this idea strongly. The avatar is present, central, and memorable. It gives the app more character. Lucida, on the other hand, feels less defined in this area.

Lucida could benefit from making the avatar more central on the homepage. The goal is not just to show a character. The goal is to make users feel:

**“There is someone here to practice with me.”**

A robot-like avatar can also be helpful because it reminds users that they are not being judged by a real person. That can make speaking feel safer.

# 4. Onboarding should follow an emotional journey

Learning English is not just about grammar, vocabulary, or lesson plans. For many users, the real problem is emotional. They may feel nervous when speaking. They may be afraid of making mistakes. They may not know how to study. They may have tried before and stopped because the process felt boring or too hard.

So before asking the user to pay, apps should make the user feel three things:

“We understand you” → “We invested in you” → “You can do this” → Paywall

so from user perspective :

- First: “This app understands my problem.” (user relates with app and solution)
- Second: “This app created something personal for me.” (anchoring user making them think that they are invested)
- Third: “I can actually do this.” (making user believing the solution)

This flow makes the paywall feel less sudden. The user is paying for a solution that feels relevant to their own situation rather than just an app.

## 4a. Giving the feeling of “we understand your problem”

Lucida should do more to show that it understands the user’s real struggle.

A user who wants to improve speaking may think:

- “I know the word, but I freeze.”
- “I translate everything in my head.”
- “I am scared of making mistakes.”
- “I do not want to sound childish.”
- “I study, but then I forget.”

These are very common feelings for language learners. If Lucida names these feelings clearly, the user can feel seen. This is important because the app starts to feel like a safe place to practice.

Lingo AI does this well. It asks users if they relate to problems like not knowing how to choose the right lessons, or giving up when the learning plan feels too hard or boring. These questions are simple, but they create a strong connection. The user feels like the app understands the real reason they struggle. Here some of the screens Lingo AI has :

- "do you relate to the statement below, I have no idea how to choose the right lessons for me" -> click yes or no
- "do you relate to the statement below, I can easily give up when my learning plan feels too hard too boring" →  click yes or no
- "Lingo ai was made for people just like you" random insan avatarları cycle ediyor. %87.3 of lingo ai users, claim that the learning plans we offer are easy to follow etc.. -> sadece geçmelik ekran
- "want smoother communication" -> click to “yes I am ready”
- "want greater success" -> click to “yes I am ready”

Lucida could add a few screens like this before creating the user’s plan.

## 4b. Giving the feeling of “this app invested in me”

Lucida already does this part quite well.

During onboarding, Lucida asks several personal questions: why the user wants to speak better English, where they need the most help, what topics they are interested in, and how much time they want to practice each day. After that, it creates a personalized plan. This helps the user feel that the app is not showing a generic path. It is building something based on their answers.

This is valuable because users are more likely to care about a plan when they feel they helped create it.

But Lucida could make this feeling even stronger by showing a clearer “plan creation” moment.

For example, after the questions, Lucida could show:

“Creating your speaking plan…”
Based on your goal, level, interests, and daily practice time.

Then it could show short loading steps:

- Analyzing your speaking goal
- Choosing topics you care about
- Setting your daily practice rhythm
- Matching you with your ideal speaking partner

This would make the user feel that Lucida has spent effort on them. That feeling matters before the paywall.

## 4c. Motivating the user before the paywall

Right now, the onboarding asks useful questions and creates a plan, but it does not clearly tell the user: “You can do this.” That is a missed opportunity.

Lingo AI handles motivation better. For example, after asking this 2 questions :

- age
- gender

it pops up an encouraging screens like “you have great potential to crush your goals”. Plus some scientific evidences shown supporting that.

# 5. Making the user’s daily practice goal visible on the homepage.

Lucida already asks users how much time they want to practice every day. This is a useful onboarding question. But after onboarding, this goal should not disappear.

A daily time goal could help users stay active.

If the user chooses 5, 10, or 15 minutes a day, the homepage can turn this into a visible daily target. This would make the homepage more motivating and easier to understand. Instead of only showing lessons, Lucida would also show the user a simple daily mission:

“Practice 10 minutes today.”

A small time bar could work very well here.

For example:

Today’s speaking goal:
3 / 10 minutes completed

The user immediately understands two things:

- They have already made progress today.
- They only need a few more minutes to complete their goal.

This makes practice feel smaller and more achievable. It also gives users a reason to continue even when they do not want to start a full lesson.

This is especially important for a speaking app. Speaking progress is hard to feel day by day. A user may not notice that their confidence is improving after one short session. But they can clearly see that they practiced for 5 minutes today.

After the user completes the daily goal, Lucida could show a small celebration:

“Daily goal completed.”
You practiced speaking for 10 minutes today.

This should stay simple. It should not feel like pressure. The goal is not to make the user feel guilty for missing a day. The goal is to make daily practice feel clear, measurable, and rewarding.

The time bar can also help reduce confusion on the homepage. If Lucida has multiple options like roadmap lessons, casual talk, and songs, the daily goal can act as a unifying layer. No matter which activity the user chooses, the app can show that it contributes to today’s progress.

For example:

- Roadmap lesson → adds to daily speaking time
- Casual Talk → adds to daily speaking time
- Roleplay → adds to daily speaking time
- Songs → may add to listening or vocabulary time

This makes user dont need to think, “Which feature should I use now?” Instead, they can think, “Which activity helps me complete my goal today?”

The main idea is simple:

Lucida should make the user’s daily practice goal visible on the homepage.

# 6. Lucida should add a streak counter

Streaks are a familiar pattern in learning apps. They work because they give users a simple reason to return.

A streak counter on the homepage would help Lucida feel more gamified and habit-based. It would also match the roadmap structure better.

The strongest version would include two parts:

First, when the user opens the app after practicing, they see a short streak animation.

Then, on the homepage, the streak counter increases.

This creates a small reward loop. It makes the user feel that returning to the app matters.

Pingo and Learna already use small streak counters, but there is still room for Lucida to make this interaction more satisfying.

# 7. The product should feel more experience-based

Practicing experience in Lucida (and other apps) can become much stronger if speaking practice feels less like a normal lesson and more like a real situation.

Right now, many AI speaking apps use a similar format: the avatar explains the topic, asks the user to say something, gives feedback, and then moves to the next line. This works, but it can feel artificial.

For example, if the lesson is about buying a train ticket, the experience should not only be:

“Today we will practice buying a train ticket. Now say this sentence.”

A better version would place the user inside the situation.

The user opens the lesson and feels like they are at a train station. There is a ticket seller. The user has a goal: buy a ticket to a specific city. The Lucida avatar is still there, but instead of being the whole experience, it acts like a helper.

![](posts/image/lucida-app-onboarding-analysis/10.png)

This would make the practice feel more natural. The user is not just repeating sentences. They are trying to complete a small real-life task.

This creates a much better learning loop. The user is still practicing speaking, but now there is context. The conversation has a reason. The user knows why they are speaking.

This could also make Lucida’s roadmap more exciting. Each roadmap item could feel like a small experience instead of just another lesson.

Lucida can still keep Free Talk as a separate mode. Free Talk is useful when the user wants open-ended speaking practice. But roadmap lessons can be more structured and experience-based.

So the app can have two clear speaking modes:

- Free Talk : Open conversation with the AI speaking partner.
- Scenario Practice : A guided real-life situation with a clear goal.

This would also reduce confusion on the homepage. “Casual Talk” would clearly mean open conversation. Roadmap nodes would clearly mean structured speaking experiences. The user would understand the difference immediately.

# Final thoughts

Lucida is already one of the most successful consumer apps coming out of Turkey, and that makes this analysis more interesting.

Lucida already has the hard part: a useful product in a growing category.

The next opportunity is not about changing the whole product. It is about making the experience clearer and more emotionally connected.

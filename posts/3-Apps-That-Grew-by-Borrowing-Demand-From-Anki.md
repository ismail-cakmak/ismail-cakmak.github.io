# 3 Apps That Grew by Riding App Store Search Demand

Some apps do not grow through viral TikToks, big ad campaigns, or influencer pushes.

They grow quietly.

A user searches for a known app or a familiar study keyword. Another app appears nearby. The user clicks, downloads, and maybe pays.

That seems to be the growth pattern behind three flashcard apps: **Cogni**, **Noji**, and **Mopiq**.

They are different products, but they all point to the same lesson: App Store search can still be a serious growth channel when an app positions itself around keywords people already search for.

## 1. Cogni: fast growth through keyword positioning

Cogni is a flashcard learning app with ready-made topics like languages, medicine, anatomy, exam prep, math, law, geography, and computing. It launched in late 2024 and quickly reached meaningful download and revenue numbers.

[IMAGE: Cogni App Store page showing the ready-made flashcard categories]

What stands out is that there is not much visible social media activity behind the growth.

No major TikTok presence.
No obvious Meta ads.
No large affiliate or UGC push.
Only a small Instagram presence.

That makes the growth look much more search-driven.

The key move seems to be ASO around the word **Anki**. In some country stores, Cogni’s title included phrases like **“Study Anki Flashcards”**. In the US store, the current title does not include Anki, but other country versions still appear to use that keyword.

[IMAGE: Side-by-side screenshots of Cogni’s App Store title in different countries, one with “Anki” and one without it]

This suggests a simple but powerful strategy:

Cogni did not need to create demand from zero.
It placed itself near demand that already existed.

People searching for Anki or flashcard-related terms were already high-intent users. Cogni used title keywords, localization, and Apple Search Ads to show up in front of them.

[IMAGE: Screenshot showing Cogni among top advertisers for an Anki-related keyword]

Cogni also benefits from not being only a generic flashcard app. It covers specific study categories like medicine, anatomy, Japanese, and exam prep. These niches likely help it rank for more specific keywords beyond just “flashcards”.

The lesson from Cogni:

**A new app can grow quickly if it finds an existing search market and positions itself close to that demand.**

## 2. Noji: the mature version of the same playbook

Noji is a more established flashcard app. It has been live since 2021 and appears to have built a strong business from App Store search.

[IMAGE: Noji AppFigures screenshot showing downloads, revenue, and country split]

The interesting part is the country split. The US seems to drive a lot of downloads, but Germany appears to generate a large share of revenue. That is important because it shows that search demand is not equal in every country. Some markets may download more, while others may monetize better.

Noji’s growth also does not look heavily social-driven.

There is an Instagram account, but not enough activity to explain the growth.
There is a TikTok account, but it does not look like the main channel.
There is no obvious large-scale paid social or UGC engine.

Again, the stronger explanation is ASO.

At one point, Noji appeared under the name **“Anki Pro: Study Flashcards.”** Its App Store subtitles also used competitor and study keywords like **Gizmo**, **Knowt**, **Quizlet**, and localized learning terms.

[IMAGE: Historical Noji listing showing “Anki Pro: Study Flashcards”]

The more advanced part is localization.

Noji does not appear to use the same title and subtitle everywhere. In Germany, for example, the listing uses German study keywords like **“Karteikarten Lernen”** and **“Lern App.”**

[IMAGE: Noji German App Store listing showing localized title and subtitle keywords]

This is a much better approach than simple translation.

Good localization means asking:

What do students in this country actually search for?
Which competitor names are familiar here?
Which generic terms have demand?
Which terms convert into paying users?

Noji also appears in strong positions for keywords like Anki, Quizlet, Knowt, flashcards, and related study terms.

[IMAGE: Noji keyword ranking screenshot showing top-5 keyword rankings]

The lesson from Noji:

**ASO becomes more powerful when it is localized country by country instead of copied across every market.**

## 3. Mopiq: the smaller solo-developer version

Mopiq is an AI flashcard app that shows the smaller version of this same strategy.

It does not appear as large as Noji or Cogni, but it still has meaningful revenue. That makes it interesting because the strategy does not only work for bigger studios.

[IMAGE: Mopiq AppFigures screenshot showing revenue, downloads, and country split]

Mopiq also appears around Anki-related search ads. In one screenshot, it shows up as a top advertiser for an Anki-related keyword.

[IMAGE: Mopiq Apple Search Ads screenshot showing it as a top advertiser]

But Mopiq seems weaker on organic keyword ranking compared with Noji. Its app description does not strongly lean into Anki-related terms, and its keyword rankings appear less dominant.

[IMAGE: Mopiq keyword ranking screenshot showing weaker rankings]

This makes Mopiq a useful contrast.

Noji looks like the stronger ASO machine.
Cogni looks like the fast new entrant.
Mopiq looks like the leaner version that still captures enough demand to make real money.

That is an important point for indie developers.

You do not always need to dominate the category.
You need to find enough high-intent search traffic to make the app profitable.

The lesson from Mopiq:

**Even a smaller app can build a real business by capturing a slice of existing search demand.**

## The shared growth pattern

These three apps all point to the same growth loop.

First, they find a search term with existing demand.
Then they position the app near that term through title, subtitle, or localization.
Then they use Apple Search Ads to appear in front of the same high-intent users.
If the app monetizes, that revenue can fund more search visibility.

That creates a simple loop:

Search demand brings downloads.
Downloads bring revenue.
Revenue funds ads.
Ads support rankings and visibility.
Visibility brings more downloads.

[IMAGE: Simple loop diagram: Search demand → Downloads → Revenue → Apple Search Ads → More visibility]

This is not flashy growth.
It is not viral.
It does not look exciting from the outside.

But it can work very well.

---

## The risk: going too close to another brand

There is a clear risk here.

Using a competitor’s brand name too directly can confuse users. It can also create trust problems. Some users may feel misled if they think they are downloading one app and later realize it is a different product.

That is the dangerous version of this strategy.

The better version is not “copy the brand name.”

The better version is:

Understand why people search for that brand.
Find the job they are trying to solve.
Build a focused product for that job.
Use honest keywords that describe the use case clearly.

For example, instead of building something that looks like “Anki clone”, a cleaner strategy would be:

Medical flashcards for USMLE
Japanese kanji flashcards
Flashcards for German nursing exams
Spaced repetition for law students
AI flashcards from PDFs
Exam prep flashcards for university students

These are still high-intent keywords, but they are clearer and safer.

## The main takeaway

The biggest lesson from Cogni, Noji, and Mopiq is not about flashcards.

It is about demand.

A lot of founders try to create demand from scratch. That is hard. These apps show another path: find demand that already exists, then build and position a product around it.

The App Store is still a search engine.

And niche keywords can still create real businesses.
